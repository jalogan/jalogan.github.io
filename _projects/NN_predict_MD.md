Molecular dynamics simulations require evaluating billions of particle interactions. For simple systems like Lennard–Jones, the analytic potential is known — but in proteins, soft matter, or experimental systems, the true force field is unknown or intractable. This project shows how a physics-informed neural network can learn the underlying force law directly from data, while preserving the symmetries and invariants of the system.


## **Why This Work Matters**

Molecular dynamics (MD) is one of the most widely used tools in computational science, providing a window into how physical systems evolve in time. The core idea is simple: particles experience forces from other particles, boundaries, or external fields, and those forces determine how their positions and momenta change step by step.  

In practice, these forces are computed from an assumed interaction potential, such as the Lennard–Jones model. This works well for simple systems, but in many real cases—proteins, soft matter, or complex fluids—the true potential is either unknown or expensive to evaluate. Computing all pairwise forces is also the most intensive part of MD, scaling as $$\mathcal{O}(N^2)$$ for $$N$$ particles, with additional costs for higher-body interactions.  

Neural networks open a new path: instead of hand-specifying the potential, we can ask whether a model can **learn the underlying force law directly from data**. If successful, such a surrogate could reproduce known physics where we have ground truth, and generalize to cases where the true potential is unavailable.  

**Can a neural network discover the effective force law of a system directly from trajectory data, without ever being told the analytic potential?**

Perhaps we don't need to know the analytic form of a potential in order to explore different regimes and phases of the system. Before embarking on this journey, we first need to ask, **Can a neural network learn the underlying force law directly from data, without being told the analytic form of the potential?**

## **Motivation and Assumptions**

The long-term goal is to learn force fields in complex systems (proteins, soft matter, fluids) where the true potential is unknown. As a first step, we test the idea on the well-known Lennard–Jones (LJ) system, treating it as if the potential were hidden and asking: *can a neural network discover the underlying force law from data alone?*

Key assumptions:

- **Central forces:** Particles are isotropic, so the force depends only on pair distance $$r$$, not orientation.  
- **Force form:** For particle $$i$$, the net force is written as a sum of radial contributions from its neighbors $$j$$:  

  $$
  \vec{F}_i = \sum_{j=1}^{K} f(r_{ij}) \, \hat{r}_{ij},
  $$

  where $$r_{ij} = \lVert \vec{r}_j - \vec{r}_i \rVert$$.  
- **Dimensionality reduction:** Instead of learning a full 3D vector field, the NN learns a **scalar radial function** $$f(r)$$.  
- **Symmetry preservation:** This design makes the model inherently **rotationally and translationally invariant**.  
- **Justification:** Most classical potentials (hard-sphere, LJ, Coulomb, Yukawa) share this structure, making it the least-biased assumption.  

 

{% comment %}
While the real fruit of this idea lies in complex systems such as proteins and other complex systems where we do not know the precise potential, this work tests the idea on a well-known system of Lennard-Jones (LJ) spheres. We know this form the onset, but, to some extent, we pretend to not know the exact potential and try to infer information from the symmetries of the data provided. 

While anisotropic potentials can appear naturally, the symmetry of the data suggests an isotropic potential---spherical particles have no preferred direction leaving us with separation distance $$r$$ as the only variable. A huge fraction of naturally occurring classical interaction potentials are central: hard-sphere, Lennard-Jones, Coulomb, Yukawa, to name a few. With the limited information we have from the data we discovered, assuming a central force is the least biased approach that respects the symmetry we know to be present. The assumption moving forward will be that the interaction force is central and the force on particle $$i$$ can be written as a sum of pair forces from all $$n_i$$ neighbors $$j$$

$$
\vec{F}_i = \sum\limits_{j=1}^{K} f_{ij}(r) \hat{r}_{ij}.
$$

This assumption is not only necessary, because otherwise the problem is highly unconstrained, but leads to a huge dimensionality reduction. For a general force field, each particle feels a full 3D force that depends on the full configuration of neighbors $$\vec{F}_i = f(\vec{r}_{ij_1}, \vec{r}_{ij_2}, \dots)$$. The central force assumption reduces the problem to a scalar radial function $$f(r_{ij})$$, where $$r_{ij} = \lVert \vec{r}_j - \vec{r}_i \rVert$$ and the direction is accounted for by the particle positions. $$f(r)$$ is just a 1D curve instead of a full 3D vector field. In addition, the scalar nature of the proposed force equation and because it only depends on relative distances ($$r_{ij}$$) makes it inherently **rotationally and translationally invariant**. 
{% endcomment %}


## **Data Preparation and Neural Network Architecture**

Training data was generated with the open-source MD package **LAMMPS**, simulating isotropic particles of diameter $$\sigma$$ interacting through a Lennard–Jones potential. For each configuration:  

- **Positions and forces** were extracted for all particles.  
- **Neighbor vectors** were computed using periodic boundary conditions (PBC) with a cutoff of $$2.5\sigma$$.  
- Each particle’s environment was represented by up to $$K$$ nearest neighbors, padded and masked to ensure uniform tensor shapes.  

The processed data was then loaded into **PyTorch**:  

- Force components were **Z-scored independently** (subtracting mean, dividing by standard deviation) across all particles and timesteps, centering targets near zero with unit variance.  
- For each particle $$i$$, the distance to each neighbor $$j$$ ($$r_{ij}$$) was expanded into a vector of **radial basis functions (RBFs)**. These provide a smooth and expressive representation of neighbor interactions, especially near the steep repulsive wall.  
- The RBF vectors were passed through a **multi-layer perceptron (MLP)** that outputs a scalar force magnitude for each pair.  
- The final force on particle $$i$$ was obtained by summing these magnitudes multiplied by the corresponding unit vectors $$\hat{r}_{ij}$$:  

  $$
  \vec{F}_i = \sum_{j=1}^{K} f(r_{ij}) \, \hat{r}_{ij}.
  $$  

{% comment %}
The data was prepared using the open-source MD software LAMMPS. We performed many simulations of isotropic particles with diameter $$\sigma$$ interacting through a LJ potential, and we extract per-particle positions and neighbor vectors accounting for periodic boundary conditions (PBC) and a cutoff of $$2.5\sigma$$, and forces on each particle to be used as labels. The coordination of each particle is represented by up to $$K$$ nearest neighbors, which are padded and masked for uniform input size.

The prepared data is read into PyTorch, where the per component mean and standard deviation is computed over all particles in all time steps in order to Z-score the force components independently. This centers the training targets near zero with unit variance. For every particle $$i$$, we compute the distance $$r_{ij}$$ to each of its neighbors $$j$$, and we expand it in a vector of **radial basis functions** (RBFs). The RBFs provide a smooth, expressive input representation of the neighbor interactions. The RBFs are passed to a MLP, which maps the features to the scalar force magnitude for each pair of neighbors. For each particle these force magnitudes are summed over the nearest $$K$$ neighbors to compute the predicted net force.
{% endcomment %}




## **Results**

The physics-informed neural network (NN) was trained on Lennard–Jones (LJ) trajectory data and tested on its ability to recover forces, reproduce the LJ force law, and generate stable MD trajectories. The results demonstrate that the model is capable of rediscovering the LJ interaction and preserving structural and dynamical properties of the system.

#### **TL;DR**

The physics-informed NN reproduces LJ forces with near-1:1 accuracy, recovers the LJ force–distance curve, and preserves equilibrium structure (g(r)). When plugged into an MD loop, the surrogate yields trajectories that remain close to LJ reference dynamics over long horizons, with only mild long-time drift. Loss curves show smooth, stable convergence, indicating strong generalization. See the side-by-side MD overlay and evolving $$g(r)$$.



## **Takeaways and Limitations**

This project shows that embedding physical symmetries into neural networks yields interpretable and stable surrogate force fields. The approach works well for Lennard–Jones, but also highlights the limitations of MLPs for scaling to more complex systems.

- The model accurately reproduces LJ forces but does not enforce **energy conservation** or **Newton’s third law**, leading to mild long-time drift.  
- Embedding invariants (central forces, translational/rotational symmetry) was essential for stability and interpretability.  
- MLPs are constrained by their **fixed neighbor set** and cannot naturally capture emergent many-body effects or variable system sizes.  

### **Future Work**

Extending this approach to proteins and complex fluids requires richer architectures and physics priors:

- Incorporate **multi-particle types**, **anisotropy**, and **long-range forces** (Coulomb).  
- Add **higher-order interactions** (bond angles, dihedrals) for polymers and proteins.  
- Transition to **Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)** for scalability, many-body expressivity, and built-in enforcement of Newton’s third law.  
- Apply these ideas to “holy grail” systems like **protein folding** and **complex fluids**, where analytic force fields are unavailable.  


{% comment %}
The neural network was able to learn the underlying force directly from data. To do so, it was necessary to embed the symmetries and invariants of the physics into the architecture, but, in the end, it produced interpretable and stable surrogate force fields. Scaling the system size up will lead to poorer results because of the limit on the number of neighbors accounted for, but if you choose a relatively high number it can do arbitrarily well---especially for interactions like LJ which have a cutoff. Recall this model does not include explicit energy conservation or action-reaction constraints (Newton's Third Law), and adding pair-symmetric messages or energy-based training would further reduce long-time drift.

This test work used a relatively simple potential, but the repulsive part of a LJ potential has a large gradient and can be difficult to learn. To extend the methods to more complex potentials, perhaps for protein-protein interactions or complex fluids, it would be necessary to include more physics and higher-order interactions. Anisotropic particles or polymers require 3- and 4-body interactions to capture the bond and dihedral angle degrees of freedom, they often include multiple particle types, and long-range forces (Coulomb interaction). 

To improve the method and continue to more complex systems, graph neural netwoks (GNNs) would be better suited than MLPs. MLPs have a fixed-size neighbor set ($$K$$ nearest neighbors per particle) and cannot capture emergent collective interactions. GNNs solve these problems and can directly encode bonds, angles, dihedrals, and topology through features and edges. In a GNN the particles would be modeled as nodes and the interactions as edges. Multi-hop messages enable collective interactions to be encoded, and pairwise interactions would be inherently symmetric, obeying Newton's Third Law. We can explicitly include this in the MLP, but we have not in the present work. 
{% endcomment %}
